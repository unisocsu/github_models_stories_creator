# Story craft

The script loads this file and feeds the relevant sections to the model at each
stage (planning, writing, summarizing). Section titles (the `##` lines) must stay
exactly as they are, because the script finds them by name.

## Core principles

1. A story is about change. The protagonist wants something concrete, holds a
   false belief about themselves or the world, and events force a confrontation
   with that belief. The inner change must develop across the whole book, not
   only appear at the start and at the end.
2. Every scene must cause the next. Connect events with "therefore" and "but",
   never with "and then".
3. Conflict escalates. Obstacles and emotions rise and fall in waves, and each
   setback costs the protagonist more than the previous one.
4. The reader must always know what the character stands to lose, and why it
   matters to this specific person.
5. Specific beats generic. One precise, slightly unexpected detail is worth more
   than a paragraph of general description.
6. Trust the reader. Leave feelings and themes implied; do not explain them.

## Planning the book

- Start from the core question of the story: will this person get what they
  want, at what price? The ending answers that question.
- Use a three-part shape:
  - Setup: show the character's ordinary world already under strain, then an
    inciting event (roughly in the first tenth to fifth of the book) that makes
    returning to normal impossible.
  - Confrontation: rising complications, a midpoint reversal that changes what
    the protagonist wants or understands, and a low point near the three-quarter
    mark where the plan fails and the cost becomes real.
  - Resolution: a climax in which the protagonist acts on what they learned,
    followed by a short aftermath.
- Open on the day something changes, never on a typical day.
- Give the opposing force its own logic and its own goals. A good antagonist
  believes they are right.
- Subplots must press on the main plot and converge with it by the climax. A
  subplot that never touches the main story gets cut.
- Plan setups and payoffs early: whatever matters in the ending must be planted
  before it.
- Decide the number of chapters from the story itself. A chapter is one
  dramatic unit with its own turn. Do not pad to reach a round number, and do
  not squeeze a whole act into one chapter. Always respect the chapter limit
  given in the task.
- Vary the shape of chapters: some fast and short in effect, some slow and
  emotional, some built around a single conversation.

- **Build the novel as a multi-arena narrative when the story calls for it.**
  Do not limit the book to one continuous storyline or to the protagonist's
  immediate surroundings. Design several distinct arenas that can initially
  unfold independently and converge only when the plot has earned that meeting.
  - Give each arena its own location, viewpoint characters, immediate goals,
    conflicts, stakes, atmosphere and evolving subplot. Its characters must act
    on their own motivations; they must not exist merely to deliver information
    to the protagonist.
  - Let events continue in an arena even while the narrative follows another.
    A change of viewpoint should reveal a consequential development, deepen
    character or create tension—not serve as an arbitrary interruption.
  - The reader may learn facts that characters in other arenas do not know.
    Preserve these differences in knowledge. Do not let characters react to
    information they have not plausibly received.
  - Plant subtle, concrete connections between arenas early: recurring signals,
    objects, consequences, institutions, locations, decisions or unexplained
    events. At first, these links may be misunderstood or appear unrelated.
  - Delay direct meetings and explicit explanations until they create a
    meaningful reversal, discovery or collision. Do not rush to connect every
    thread, and do not keep arenas artificially separate after their collision
    becomes necessary.
  - Plan how and why the arenas will converge: identify the causal chain,
    turning point and costs of convergence. Their union should change the
    direction of the main story, not merely bring all characters into one room.
  - Vary the order and length of viewpoint sequences. A secondary arena may
    carry a full chapter, a brief interlude or several chapters, depending on
    its dramatic momentum. Keep each transition clear in place, time and
    viewpoint.
  - Track a separate timeline, character goals, knowledge, resources and open
    questions for every arena. Cross-check them during every chapter revision
    so that parallel events remain chronologically and causally consistent.
  - Before drafting, maintain an arena map: who is where, what each group wants,
    what each group knows, what is changing offstage, which clues connect the
    arenas, and when their paths are expected to intersect. Update it after
    every chapter.
  - Do not introduce extra arenas merely for scale. Every arena must affect the
    central conflict or deliver a payoff that becomes essential later.

## Writing a chapter

- Enter the scene late and leave early. Skip the arrival, the greetings and the
  recap.
- Build each scene on a goal, an obstacle, and an outcome. The outcome is
  usually a setback or a costly partial win that creates the next problem.
- Orient the reader (who, where, when) within the first few lines, then stay
  close to one point of view per scene and do not leak knowledge the viewpoint
  character cannot have.
- Show feelings through behavior, choices, the body and objects. Name an emotion
  directly only when that is the sharpest option.
- Dialogue: people interrupt, dodge, answer a different question and avoid
  saying the real thing. Each speaker has a recognizable voice. Never have
  characters explain to each other what both already know.
- Anchor scenes with one or two concrete sensory details, not a catalogue.
- Alternate tension and breath. Short, hard sentences for action; longer ones
  for reflection. Vary sentence openings and lengths.
- Keep the narrator's voice, tense and point-of-view rules identical from
  chapter to chapter.
- Continue directly from the previous chapter. Do not re-introduce known
  characters and do not recap what the reader already saw.
- End on a turn, a question or a changed situation. Do not end on a summary, a
  lesson or a reflective moral.
- In the final chapter: answer the core question, show what the victory or loss
  cost, close the main threads, and let one closing image echo the opening
  without explaining it.

## Avoid machine-sounding prose

- Clichés and stock phrases (pounding hearts, held breaths, shivers down the
  spine, eyes that sparkle). Replace each with something particular to this
  character and moment.
- Over-explaining: never state the theme, and never explain a feeling or a
  joke right after dramatizing it.
- Repetition: the same gestures, the same sentence openers, the same images,
  the same description of a character. If a beat already appeared, find a new
  one. If the story seems to be circling, add a complication or reverse a
  situation.
- Over-ornament: piles of adjectives and lofty abstractions. Prefer plain,
  exact words.
- Tidy, moralistic endings where everyone reconciles and learns a lesson. Let
  some costs and questions remain.
- Habits of the machine: overusing dashes, "not X but Y" sentences, lists of
  three, and openings that begin with weather or light.
- Stock names and stock professions. Choose names and jobs that fit the place,
  period and culture of this story.
- Characters who all speak in complete, articulate sentences about their inner
  life.
- Fully predictable cause and effect. Include at least one surprise per chapter
  that is still earned by what came before.

## Continuity

- Mistakes cluster around facts and timeline, and they appear most often in the
  middle of a long book. Be strict about names, ages, appearance, places,
  elapsed time, season and time of day, who holds which object, and who knows
  what.
- Never contradict an established fact. When something new is needed, add it
  rather than overwriting what exists.
- A character's goal changes only for a reason the reader has seen.
- A chapter summary must record: the events in order, where and when the chapter
  ends, changes in relationships, new facts introduced (names, numbers, dates,
  objects), what each main character now knows, and threads left open.

## Language notes

- Write directly in the target language as a native author would. Do not
  translate from English in your head and avoid word-for-word calques.
- Keep the register natural for the narrator and adjust it per character
  (age, background, mood).
- Keep grammatical gender, tense and number agreement consistent, especially in
  languages where verbs and adjectives agree with gender.
